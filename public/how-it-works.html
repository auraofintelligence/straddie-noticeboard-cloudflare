How the network would work

Each notice would have one record with its source, permissions and expiry. The web board, standard displays and touchscreens would all use that record so people can check the source and see when the information changes.

Follow the Dunwich island arts night invitation

In this fictional example, the organiser plans live original music and a short captioned film about local printmaking, painting and music, told in the artists’ own voices. Follow the invitation as it goes from draft to display, then changes time.

  1. Gather the information

    The organiser plans a 6.30 pm Saturday start in the community hall courtyard in Dunwich. Confirm the music and screening programme, where people should arrive and which access details the venue can verify. A real invitation would link to the organiser’s public event page.

  2. Verify the source

    Check the time, entrance and programme with the organiser. Ask who can confirm a late change. Agree with each artist how to credit them and confirm permission to use their work, voice and music in the film or invitation.

  3. Prepare the notice

    Write a short invitation with the title, courtyard, time, live music, captioned film and organiser’s source. The organiser chooses which approved outlets may show it, such as the web board, a venue TV and a touchscreen kiosk.

    Try the draft tool

  4. Approve and publish

    The reviewer confirms the facts, permission, outlets and the 7.15 pm take-down time. The steward releases one approved record. A transport display would also need approval from its operator or host.

  5. Adapt for each screen

    The TV can show the title, place and time in large type. Phones can open the source, and a kiosk can show the programme or directions. The organiser’s details stay the same across the display versions.

    Preview the arts night invitation

  6. Correct or take it down

    The organiser confirms a new start time of 7 pm. Update the same record and ask each host to confirm the new time is visible. Flag any offline display for follow-up and check the invitation comes down at 7.15 pm.

What a notice needs

Details visitors can use

A short title, place, dates, who it’s for and what someone can do next. Show the publisher, a public source or contact and when it was last checked.

Information for the reviewer and host

Record the person responsible, approved channels, credits and permissions, version and take-down time. A host needs to confirm which version is on screen and whom to call for a correction.

Who would be responsible?

Luke Nathan Hayes is preparing the prototype through his business, Strange But True. He is asking local people to help form Ready SETCO, a proposed co-operative. The noticeboard is intended to be its first project. Ready SETCO’s first project proposal

The author or publisher
Provides the details, public source and permission to use the material. Supplies corrections when something changes.
Ready SETCO, if formed
The co-operative could take on agreed responsibilities after a formal handover. Its members would decide how to bring publishers, reviewers and hosts together, cover review shifts and handle complaints and corrections. Cultural material would need permission from the people with authority to share it.
The screen host
Agrees what the venue will show, names the equipment owner and maintenance contact, keeps the screen usable and checks it after a correction or outage. Businesses could choose to publish, host, or do both.
Council, agencies and transport operators
Remain the source of their own official updates. A future relay would preserve the issuer’s wording, times and area, with an agreed way to check new versions and cancellations.
